Methodology

A transparent editorial process for deciding what belongs in a living dictionary of AI-era language, what the evidence supports, and how each entry should be described.

1. Identify candidate terms

Candidate terms may come from technical documentation, research, industry usage, workplaces, media, online communities, or observed changes in how people talk about AI.

2. Establish real usage

A term should represent documented language in actual use, not merely something an AI model can generate. Emerging slang may require less formal evidence than a technical definition, but AILex should be able to show why the term belongs.

3. Classify the term

Each entry receives one or more categories and a status that helps readers understand what kind of language they are encountering: established, technical, emerging, slang, governance, research, security, contested, or deprecated.

4. Write for clarity

Definitions prioritize plain English, accuracy, and useful distinctions over hype. Each entry includes pronunciation and a natural example sentence so AILex works like a dictionary rather than a technical glossary.

5. Research provenance

AILex separates evidence about a term's meaning from evidence about its origin. A source that explains a term does not automatically prove who coined it or when it first appeared.

Where the evidence supports it, a provenance record can document who coined, introduced, formalized, or popularized a term; the earliest use AILex can responsibly establish; meaningful events in the term's development; related AILex terms; and the sources supporting those claims.

Primary sources such as original papers, specifications, announcements, documentation, and first-party posts are preferred for technical definitions, origin claims, and release dates. Secondary and reference sources are useful for broader usage, adoption, and historical context.

6. Represent uncertainty

AILex does not require every term to have a neat origin story. If the earliest use is uncertain, older than the current research scope, or contested, the field can remain unknown. Language such as coined by, introduced by, formalized by, and popularized by is used deliberately because those claims mean different things.

A term can remain published while its provenance record is marked pending. A researched record means an initial human-reviewed sourcing pass has been completed; it does not mean the history can never be revised.

7. Human review

AI may assist with candidate discovery, source discovery, research organization, drafting, coding, and editing, but final editorial inclusion and factual judgment remain human-reviewed. AI output by itself is never treated as a source.

Before a provenance claim is published as researched, the reviewer should verify that the source exists, actually supports the claim assigned to it, and does not justify stronger attribution language than the evidence permits.

8. Revisit as language changes

AILex is a living dictionary. Definitions, statuses, categories, source records, related terms, and historical notes may change as usage becomes clearer or better evidence emerges.

Current provenance phase

The initial sourcing model is being tested across different kinds of vocabulary rather than filling every entry with shallow citations at once. The first researched set includes Few-Shot, MCP, Model Collapse, Prompt Injection, RAG, and Vibe Coding. Remaining terms have explicit pending research records.

Planned evolution

The next stages include completing provenance research across the published dictionary, surfacing source citations and term history through dedicated term pages, expanding related-term relationships, adding audible pronunciation, and formalizing the editorial review lifecycle.
