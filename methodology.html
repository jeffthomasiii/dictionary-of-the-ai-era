Methodology

A transparent editorial process for deciding what belongs in a living dictionary of AI-era language, what the evidence supports, and how each entry should be described.

1. Identify candidate terms

Candidate terms may come from technical documentation, research, industry usage, workplaces, media, online communities, product interfaces, standards, or observed changes in how people talk about AI.

2. Establish real usage

A term should represent language that can be documented in actual use, not merely something an AI model can generate. Evidence expectations vary by term type, but AILex should be able to explain why a candidate deserves its own dictionary entry.

3. Decide whether the term adds reader value

Not every phrase used near AI belongs in the dictionary. AILex considers whether the concept is distinct enough to look up, whether an alias belongs under an existing entry instead, and whether a broad computing term has a meaningful AI-era use that justifies inclusion.

4. Classify the term

Each entry receives one or more categories and a status that help readers understand what kind of language they are encountering. Categories describe where the concept belongs; status communicates whether the term is established, technical, emerging, slang, governance-oriented, research-oriented, security-related, contested, or deprecated.

5. Write for clarity

Definitions prioritize plain English, accuracy, and useful distinctions over hype. Each entry includes a written pronunciation and natural example sentence. Audible pronunciation is provided where the browser supports speech synthesis, with explicit overrides for acronyms and terms likely to be misread.

6. Research provenance

AILex separates evidence about a term's meaning from evidence about its origin. A source that explains a term does not automatically prove who coined it or when it first appeared.

Where evidence supports it, provenance may document who coined, introduced, formalized, or popularized a term; the earliest use AILex can responsibly establish; meaningful events in the term's development; related entries; and the sources supporting those claims.

Primary sources such as original papers, specifications, announcements, documentation, standards, and first-party posts are preferred for technical definitions, naming events, and release dates. Secondary and reference sources can support broader usage, adoption, and historical context.

7. Represent uncertainty

AILex does not require every term to have a neat origin story. If the earliest use is uncertain, older than the available research scope, or contested, the field can remain unknown. Attribution language such as coined by, introduced by, formalized by, and popularized by is used deliberately because those claims mean different things.

8. Connect the term without inventing relationships

Related-term discovery helps readers continue through connected concepts. The current provenance schema records related entries but does not encode relationship types, so the interface does not invent labels such as “depends on,” “contrasts with,” or “is a subtype of” unless future data explicitly supports those distinctions.

9. Human review

AI may assist with candidate discovery, source discovery, research organization, drafting, coding, and editing, but final editorial inclusion and factual judgment remain human-reviewed. AI output by itself is never treated as a source.

Before a provenance record is considered researched, the reviewer verifies that its sources exist, are appropriate for the claims assigned to them, and do not justify stronger attribution language than the evidence permits.

10. Publish as part of a living reference

Published terms receive stable dedicated URLs and become part of Browse, category collections, related-term discovery, sitemap/indexing metadata, and the Living Dictionary. Definitions, statuses, categories, sources, relationships, and historical notes may later change as usage becomes clearer or better evidence appears.

Current editorial state

AILex currently contains 100 published terms, each with a matching provenance record that has completed an initial researched sourcing pass. “Researched” does not mean permanent certainty. It means the current record has been sourced and human-reviewed and may still be revised when stronger evidence emerges.

The 100-term corpus is an MVP floor rather than a fixed annual limit. Future entries are added according to editorial value, documented usage, and coverage gaps.

Living Dictionary and annual editions

The public site is the continuously updated AILex Living Dictionary. Named annual editions are immutable snapshots created at a declared editorial cutoff for historical reference and citation.

What evolves next

Near-term work is focused less on adding interface features and more on quality: continuing corpus expansion, documentation maintenance, cross-device and accessibility QA, stronger contribution/review processes as participation grows, and deliberate brand/domain decisions. Richer relationship semantics, timelines, curated audio, and automated candidate discovery remain possible later improvements rather than MVP requirements.
